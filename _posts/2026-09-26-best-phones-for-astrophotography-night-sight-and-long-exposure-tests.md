## How phone astrophotography works

Stars are faint, so a camera needs to gather light for a long time. A traditional camera leaves its shutter open for 20–30 seconds. Phones take a different approach:

1. They capture **many shorter exposures** (often 10–16 seconds each) over a few minutes.
2. Software **aligns and stacks** them, averaging out noise while keeping real detail.
3. It removes **hot pixels** and balances the sky's colour.

Google popularised this with **Astrophotography mode** in Night Sight on Pixel phones, and most major brands now have a similar mode that activates when the phone detects it's on a tripod.

## Features that matter most

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Large main sensor** | Collects more light per exposure |
| **Wide aperture** (f/1.6–f/1.9) | More light in each second |
| **Dedicated astro mode** | Handles stacking and exposure automatically |
| **Pro/manual mode with 30s shutter** | Full control for experienced users |
| **RAW (DNG) capture** | Lets you edit noise and colour properly |
| **Good ultra-wide** | Fits more of the Milky Way in the frame |

Rather than a strict ranking that dates quickly, here are the families that consistently perform well in night-sky tests:

## Your first Milky Way shot: step by step

1. **Find darkness.** Get away from city lights and check a light-pollution map. A moonless night is best.
2. **Use a tripod** or wedge the phone on a rock. Any movement ruins long exposures.
3. **Turn off the flash and screen brightness** to protect your night vision.
4. **Open Night or Astro mode**, or Pro mode with ISO 800–3200 and the longest shutter available.
5. **Focus at infinity.** Tap on a bright star or set manual focus to the mountain/infinity symbol.
6. **Use a timer** (3 seconds) so tapping the shutter doesn't shake the phone.
7. **Wait.** Astro modes can take 1–4 minutes. Don't move the phone.

## Editing tips

- Shoot **RAW** if available and edit in Lightroom Mobile or Snapseed.
- Raise **exposure** and **contrast** gently; reduce **highlights** to keep stars sharp.
- Cool the **white balance** (around 3,800–4,500K) for natural-looking skies.
- Apply noise reduction lightly, because too much turns stars into mush.
